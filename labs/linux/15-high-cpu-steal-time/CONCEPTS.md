# Lab 15 — Concept: CPU Steal Time

## What's actually going on

A virtual CPU (vCPU) isn't a physical core dedicated to your VM — it's a schedulable thread on the host, just like any other process the hypervisor's scheduler has to give time to. When your guest kernel wants to run, its vCPU thread has to actually get picked by the host's scheduler (on KVM, that's the host's CFS — Completely Fair Scheduler — treating your vCPU thread the same as it treats every other process on the box). If the host is oversubscribed — more vCPUs promised across all its guests than physical cores available, or one noisy-neighbor VM hammering its allocation — your vCPU thread sits in the host's run queue, runnable but not running, waiting its turn.

Steal time is how the guest finds out about that wait. The guest kernel can't directly observe the host's scheduler, so hypervisors expose a paravirtualized accounting channel for exactly this: KVM guests get it via a shared memory region the host periodically updates through `MSR_KVM_STEAL_TIME` (Xen and VMware have their own equivalent mechanisms). The guest kernel reads that region and reports the delta as `%st` in `vmstat`/`top` or `%steal` in `mpstat`. Conceptually it means: "this much wall-clock time passed where my vCPU was ready and wanted to run, but the host gave that physical core to something else instead." Crucially, this is fundamentally different from `%wa`/iowait, which means the CPU *was* scheduled but the process was blocked waiting on a disk or network completion — steal means the CPU was never handed to you in the first place, so no in-guest process, no `top` hog, no application tuning can explain it or fix it. It's invisible from inside the guest by design, because the thing causing it (whatever else is running on the physical host) isn't inside the guest.

This is also why looking only at the aggregate `%st`/`all` row in `mpstat -P ALL` output can hide the exact problem you're looking for. That aggregate is a simple arithmetic mean across every vCPU. If seven of your eight vCPUs are healthy and one is being starved at 68% steal, averaging dilutes it down to under 10% system-wide — easy to write off as noise. But if your workload has anything pinned or effectively single-threaded (a MySQL thread doing critical replication apply work, an interrupt handler bound to one queue, any single-threaded hot path), landing on that one starved core is enough to tank real performance while every system-wide number looks calm. This is the same principle as averaging away an outlier in any dataset — the mean erases exactly the signal you needed.

It's also worth being precise about what steal time is *not*: a high run queue (`r` in `vmstat`) by itself doesn't mean CPU scarcity, and neither does high load average. Both `wa` (iowait) and `st` (steal) can inflate the run queue the same way a genuine CPU shortage does — processes pile up waiting their turn regardless of *why* they're waiting. You have to read `us`/`sy` (real compute), `wa` (blocked on I/O), and `st` (blocked by the hypervisor) together to know which layer actually owns the fix.

## Where this shows up in the real world

Steal time is a fact of life on any shared-tenancy infrastructure — public cloud, budget VPS providers, or your own oversold Proxmox/KVM/VMware host. It commonly appears as "the app feels randomly slow, nobody changed any code, and nothing in `top` explains it" — exactly the kind of ticket that burns hours of application- and database-level tuning before someone runs `vmstat 1` and sees `st` sitting at 30-60%. The fix is never inside the guest: it's moving the VM to less-contended infrastructure, requesting a dedicated-core instance, or (if you own the hypervisor) reducing oversubscription or pinning vCPUs to physical cores so scheduling becomes predictable. The engineering value of recognizing `%st` fast — and knowing to check the per-core `mpstat -P ALL` breakdown, not just the aggregate — is turning a multi-day wild goose chase through app and database configs into a five-minute "this isn't our layer, escalate to infra" call.
